HistoLens

Classifies colorectal cancer tissue from microscope images into 9 tissue types.

Trained on one hospital's slides, tested on 7,180 tiles from a different clinical center.

Try the live demoCode on GitHub

The problem

Pathologists classify tissue under the microscope to diagnose and grade colorectal cancer. Models that help them usually report accuracy on images from the same source they were trained on.

Real deployments see slides from other labs, stained with other protocols and scanned with other scanners. HistoLens is trained on one center's slides and evaluated on another's.

How it works

  • Compared four models under the same compute budget: a small CNN, ResNet-18 from scratch, and ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 with and without stain augmentation.
  • Implemented H&E stain augmentation from scratch: colour deconvolution into hematoxylin, eosin and residual channels, random rescaling of each stain, then back to RGB.
  • Measured calibration on the other hospital, tried temperature scaling, and added a referral threshold so uncertain tiles go to a human.
  • Explained predictions with occlusion maps, and served the model with ONNX Runtime so the app needs no PyTorch.

Results

ModelSame hospitalOther hospital
Small CNN, from scratch96.7%95.4%
ResNet-18, from scratch93.7%92.3%
ResNet-18 pretrained, no stain augmentation98.5%95.1%
ResNet-18 pretrained + stain augmentation97.8%95.7%

What I learned

Validation accuracy picks the wrong model

Without stain augmentation the pretrained ResNet scores 98.5% on same-hospital data, the best of all, but drops to 95.1% on the other hospital, behind even the small CNN.

Stain augmentation helps, modestly

On the other hospital it fixes 171 tiles and breaks 124 (+0.6 points, McNemar p = 0.007). One training run per model, so more seeds would be needed to claim more.

Referring uncertain cases pays off

Answering only the 90% most confident tiles raises accuracy from 95.7% to 98.7%.

Limitations

  • Tiles, not whole slides: a clinical tool would aggregate thousands of tiles per patient.
  • 64×64 pixels per tile and only two medical centers.
  • No out-of-distribution check: a non-tissue image still gets one of the 9 labels.
  • Research project on public data (PathMNIST), not a medical device.
